Philadelphia

What happened

  • First cases appeared in mid-September 1918.
  • On 28 September the city went ahead with a Liberty Loan war-bonds parade — around 200,000 people packed the streets.
  • Schools, churches and theatres were not closed until early October, more than two weeks after the first cases.
  • Within days the hospitals were full. Bodies were left in homes because there were no coffins and no gravediggers.

The number to remember

Philadelphia's peak weekly death rate was roughly eight times St Louis's. Its total death rate was about double.

St Louis

What happened

  • First cases appeared in early October 1918.
  • Within about two days the city closed schools, theatres, churches, pool halls and banned public gatherings.
  • The measures held for weeks, then were relaxed in November — and cases climbed again, so some were reinstated.
  • The health commissioner was criticised at the time for over-reacting.

Why scientists still use this pair

Same virus, same season, same country. The main thing that differed was how many days each city waited. That is about as close to a controlled experiment as history ever gives us.

Australia's turn — and why it was different

Australia closed its ports to infected ships from October 1918. Maritime quarantine held the flu off until January 1919, months after it had torn through Europe and America. When it finally arrived, NSW closed schools, shut theatres and made masks compulsory in Sydney. Australia ended up with one of the lowest death rates of any developed country — roughly 15,000 deaths, when the toll could have been far worse.

Quarantine did not stop the flu. It bought time — and time was worth more than anything else available in 1918.

The strangest thing about 1918: the W

Ordinary flu kills the very young and the very old. The 1918 strain also killed healthy adults aged 20 to 40, giving the age-mortality graph an unusual W shape.

Why? Scientists still argue about it

  • People born before about 1890 may have met a similar flu as children and kept some immunity.
  • A strong immune system may have over-reacted, flooding the lungs — young adults had the strongest immune systems.
  • Crowded army camps and troop ships put young adults together in exactly the conditions flu likes.

This is a live scientific argument, not a settled fact. New evidence could still change the answer — which is the whole point.
