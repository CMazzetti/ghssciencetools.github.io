Antibiotics: How They Work

Year 8 Science — what an antibiotic actually breaks, and why that only works on bacteria

A bacterium has parts an antibiotic can break

Click a labelled part of the cell, or pick an antibiotic below. Watch what stops working.

So why doesn't it damage you?

This is the whole trick. It is called selective toxicity — the drug can only harm something that has the part it attacks.

What that means for each drug

  • Penicillin attacks a cell wall. Your cells have never had one, so there is nothing for it to attack.
  • Tetracycline fits into a bacterial ribosome the way a key fits a lock. Your ribosomes are a different shape, so the key does not turn.
  • Ciprofloxacin jams an enzyme that bacteria use to copy DNA. Your cells use a different enzyme to do the same job.

No antibiotic is perfectly selective, which is why side effects happen — including killing the useful bacteria in your gut.

And why do they do nothing to a virus?

Because a virus is not a cell. There is nothing there to break.

Check it against the three targets

  • No cell wall to weaken.
  • No ribosomes — a virus hijacks your ribosomes to build its proteins.
  • No chemical reactions of its own to jam.

This is why an antibiotic will not touch a cold, the flu or chickenpox. Taking one anyway does nothing for you and helps resistant bacteria spread.

The three mechanisms side by side

AntibioticTargetWhat goes wrongKills or stops?
PenicillinCell wall The bacterium cannot patch its wall while dividing. Water pushes in and the cell bursts. Kills
TetracyclineRibosomes Protein building stops, so the cell cannot grow, repair itself or reproduce. Stops
CiprofloxacinDNA copying DNA cannot be copied, so the cell cannot divide into two. Kills

Drugs that kill outright are called bactericidal. Drugs that only stop bacteria growing are bacteriostatic — your immune system finishes those ones off, which is why they need a longer course.
